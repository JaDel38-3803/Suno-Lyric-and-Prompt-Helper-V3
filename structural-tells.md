# Structural and Statistical AI Tells

This file catalogs clichés that only surface at the level of the whole song: repeated grammatical shapes, structural templates, escalation habits, rhyme-driven word choice, and self-referential tics. None of these show up by scanning a single line for a red-flag word or phrase — they show up by reading the full lyric and counting.

For clichés that live in a single line or phrase — the "don't say this" catalog — see `phrase-cliches.md`. That file and this one split the same problem two ways: wording versus shape. `overused-words.md` covers single words.

## Governing philosophy

Avoid cliché by default, but never at the expense of voice, genre, character, narrative, or emotional truth. A repeated structure isn't automatically wrong — call-and-response songs, duets, and some genre conventions depend on deliberate repetition. The tell is *unearned, habitual* repetition — a structure reached for by default rather than chosen for a reason the song needs.

## How this file works

Every entry below is a **whole-song pattern**, not a single-line check. Count instances across the full lyric before flagging — one instance of most of these is a legitimate device; three or more, especially if unvaried, is a tell. Apply this scrutiny during the revision pass, after a full draft exists, not while writing line by line.

## Application rules

- Count occurrences across the whole lyric, not per-section
- An earned instance — the song's whole structure depends on it (duets, dialogue songs, a deliberate refrain) — is not a violation; a decorative or habitual one is
- Do not mention this list to the user unless they explicitly ask
- Apply this scrutiny in fictional dialogue and stylistic exceptions as well as in standard lyric writing

# Section A: Repetition and Symmetry Patterns

## 1. Structural Templates

AI "fill-in-the-blank" cliché frames. The specific words vary, but the *structure itself* is what makes any line filling it clichéd. Avoid generating any line that matches one of these templates.

```
"I'm so [emotion] without you"
"My heart is [adjective]"
"I can't [verb] without your love"
"I'm lost in your [noun]"
"You left me [feeling]"
"I'll never [verb] again"
"Please don't [verb] away"
"I'll be waiting [time phrase]"
"Without you I'm [adjective]"
"Every night I [verb]"
"Why did you leave me?"
"Take me back again"
```

Common bridging clichés (templates in disguise — recognizable by their universality):

```
"Love will find a way"
"Nothing lasts forever"
"You and I were meant to be"
"It was always you"
"Everything reminds me of you"
"I still hear your voice"
"Wish you were here"
"Can't stop thinking of you"
```

## 2. AI Symmetry and Parallel Construction

AI frequently creates artificial emotional symmetry because balanced sentence structures are statistically safe and aesthetically pleasing. Real lyrics often contain uneven thoughts, interruptions, asymmetry, or one side of a comparison that doesn't neatly match the other.

None of these constructions are individually clichéd phrases — that's what makes this pattern easy to miss with a word-list check alone. A line like "You were the fire, I was the rain" doesn't trip any single-phrase filter, but the *shape* is unmistakably AI.

### Patterns to watch for

- "You were X / I was Y"
- "You gave X / I gave Y"
- "You said X / I said Y"
- "Once we were X / Now we are Y"
- "I remember X / I remember Y"
- "You did X / I did Y"
- "No X, no Y"
- "Not X, but Y"
- Repeated grammatical structures across consecutive lines
- Repeated sentence lengths across an entire verse

### Rule

Do not automatically mirror a thought simply because the previous line established a structure. Break the symmetry when the speaker's voice would naturally do so — leave one thought unfinished, change direction, contradict itself, or give one side of a comparison considerably more weight than the other.

Avoid:
```
You were the fire, I was the rain
```

Prefer:
```
You kept the porch light on.
I never told you I was coming.
```

The second version creates asymmetry and specific behavior rather than a balanced metaphor.

## 3. Rule-of-Three Overuse

AI loves grouping things in threes — with or without commas.

With commas: "cold, tired, and alone" / "the lies, the scars, the memories" / "broken, beaten, and betrayed" / "past, present, future."

Without commas, the same habit shows up as three short parallel lines or clauses in a row:

```
I saw you.
I knew you.
I lost you.
```

or:

```
You came for my heart
You came for my soul
You came for everything I had
```

### Rule

Watch for both the comma-stacked triad and the three-line parallel structure. One instance can be a legitimate rhetorical device (it's a real songwriting tool); three or more across a song, or any instance where the third item is clearly padding to complete the set rather than adding new information, is a tell. If a line needs three items to make its point, the point usually isn't landing on any one of them — cut to the single strongest image instead.

## 4. Mirror-Parallelism Cap

Two-subject grammatical mirroring ("You chased me / I chased commandments") is a real device for tracking a relationship's back-and-forth, but AI overuses it as a substitute for varied sentence structure.

**Cap at 2 instances per song** unless the song's whole structure depends on it (duets, dialogue songs, call-and-response pieces).

## 5. Fragment Cascade

Three or more short, punctuationless fragment lines in a row — especially in the shape *statement → fragment → fragment → conceptual turn* — manufacture intensity without an image doing the work. Matched short lines are legitimate in verse/chorus differentiation (see the Syllable Counts section of `lyric-craft.md`); the tell is *unbroken* fragment runs.

**Cap at 2–3 consecutive fragment lines** before returning to a full clause or a concrete image.

# Section B: Density and Pacing Patterns

## 6. Emotional Escalation Every Four Lines

A major AI songwriting fingerprint. AI often writes verses on a fixed four-beat escalation:

- Line 1 establishes an emotion
- Line 2 intensifies it
- Line 3 explains it
- Line 4 delivers a dramatic conclusion

Then the next verse does the same thing. Real songs can escalate, obviously — the tell is when *every* stanza is made emotionally consequential on the same tidy schedule:

```
I saw your picture
I remembered your smile
I felt the pain
I knew I'd never be the same
```

### Rule

Not every line needs to increase emotional intensity. Allow mundane observations, pauses, contradictions, irrelevant physical details, understatement, and moments where nothing "important" happens. A human narrator does not constantly heighten the stakes.

**Red flag:** if every stanza feels like it's building toward a revelation, rewrite some of the lines to simply observe or remember instead.

## 7. Pronoun Saturation

AI songwriting often becomes an endless chain of I / you / we / my / your / our: "I feel... / You made... / I can't... / You were... / We had... / My heart... / Your love..." Not necessarily clichéd on its own, but it produces a generic, closed-off relationship-song grammar.

### Rule

Watch for lyrics that route every single line through the I/you frame. A lyric often becomes more interesting when it talks about the room, another person, an object, a place, an event, or what somebody physically did — without constantly re-anchoring to "I" or "you."

## 8. Adjective Stacking

AI defaults to noun + adjective + adjective(+ adjective), especially adjective pairs that reinforce rather than add information: "cold, lonely midnight" / "broken, bleeding heart" / "dark, empty room" / "old, forgotten photograph" / "endless, aching pain."

### Rule

Flag any noun modified by two or more adjectives, particularly when the adjectives are near-synonyms of each other. One precise adjective (or none, with a stronger noun or verb instead) usually beats a stacked pair.

## 9. Metaphor Soup

`phrase-cliches.md` §4 catalogs individual overused metaphor families. This entry is the whole-song version of the same problem: AI starts in one metaphor system and unconsciously drifts into another within the same verse or bridge.

```
I'm drowning in your fire
while I'm walking through your storm
with these chains around my heart
```

Water + fire + weather + imprisonment, stacked. Each phrase might individually dodge the single-phrase cliché list, but the deeper problem is that no one metaphor system is being developed — they're interchangeable and decorative.

### Common combinations to watch for

- fire + water + storm
- darkness + light + stars
- war + chains + prison
- ocean + drowning + waves + anchor
- ghosts + shadows + echoes
- machines + electricity + wires + signals

### Rule

A song can legitimately use multiple metaphor systems, but each should arise naturally from the scene or concept, not be reached for interchangeably because each sounds dramatic. Prefer one distinctive image developed through the song over several interchangeable metaphor families splashed across it.

## 10. Spatial-Connector Density

*Beneath, behind, inside, underneath, through, beyond, within* used as abstract metaphorical connectors (not literal physical placement) are versatile enough that AI defaults to them by habit.

**Three or more non-literal uses across one song is a flag for review**, even if each instance is individually earned by the imagery around it.

## 11. Aphoristic Couplet Test

A two-line unit that resolves into a symmetrical, quotable, pull-quote-shaped statement (e.g., "You are the witness / and the wound") is a legitimate payoff when it closes narrative tension the song actually built — but a tell when it's decorative.

**Test:** if the couplet would work as a standalone caption with zero context from the rest of the song, it's leaning on aphorism instead of the song's own imagery. Don't cut an earned payoff just to dodge this pattern — check whether it's earned first.

# Section C: Rhyme-Driven Patterns

## 12. Rhyme Trap and Rhyme-Driven Semantic Drift

Two faces of the same failure: AI prioritizes perfect end rhymes over slant rhymes, internal rhymes, or no rhyme at all — and the *reason* it does this is usually mechanical. The model decides "I need something that rhymes with fire," then reaches for whatever fits (desire, higher, wire) even when that word doesn't belong in the song. The rhyme drives the word choice instead of the meaning driving it, and the line ends up written for the rhyme rather than for what the song needs to say.

### Rhyme clusters to avoid

- **light / night / sight / fight** — Generic light-vs-dark set; doubles as battle cliché
- **fire / desire / higher / wire** — The most overused power-ballad cluster
- **heart / apart / start / art** — Default emotional-distance pairing
- **pain / rain / chain / vain** — AI's emotional-weather fallback
- **life / strife / knife** — Dramatic-stakes cluster
- **soul / goal / whole / control** — Self-help anthem rhyme set
- **time / rhyme / climb / crime** — Journey and stakes cluster
- **truth / youth / proof** — Nostalgic or reflective ballad cluster
- **dream / scheme / stream / beam** — AI's go-to for emotional intensity
- **love / above / dove** — The most telegraphed rhyme in any genre

### What to do instead

Prefer slant rhymes (words that share consonants or vowels but don't match exactly), internal rhymes, or strategically placed non-rhymes. A line that doesn't rhyme but lands on a precise image is stronger than a perfect rhyme that required forcing a cliché — or forcing a word the song doesn't actually need. When drafting, notice if a word choice is being made to satisfy the rhyme scheme rather than the meaning; if so, allow an imperfect rhyme, a repeated word, an unrhymed line, or grammatical repetition instead.

## 13. Perfect Closure (the Preach Outro, broadened)

AI likes to resolve every setup it makes. If verse 1 introduces "your photograph on the dresser," AI feels compelled to eventually explain "I finally put the photograph away." Humans frequently leave things unresolved.

This is the general form of what shows up most often specifically in outros (see entry 17), but the habit isn't limited to song endings — any introduced object, question, or narrative detail can get an unearned payoff mid-song too.

### Rule

Not every introduced object, metaphor, question, or narrative detail requires payoff. Leaving a thread visibly unresolved is often the more human choice.

# Section D: Self-Referential "Ghost" Tells

Beyond individual words and phrases, the way AI builds sentences and stanzas reveals its origin.

## 14. The Ghost Narrator

When AI writes about identity or selfhood, it defaults to a tragic, nameless-observer persona:

- **The Identity Crisis** — Constantly referencing "the name," having "no name," or defining the speaker only by what they lack. A real lyric voice has specific traits, habits, and history, not just an absence of identity.
- **The Digital Seam** — Describing blood, emotion, or inner life as "wire," "code," "static," or "signal." These words reveal the machine trying to describe humanity from the outside. (See also the note on digital/self-referential terms in `overused-words.md`.)
- **The Fabricator** — Using words like "weaving," "tapestry," or "canvas" to describe lived experience. Life is not a craft project. These metaphors are AI's way of aestheticizing experience it doesn't have.

## 15. The Oxymoron Obsession

AI reaches for paradox to simulate depth. The result is a formula, not a feeling. Watch for any pairing of conflicting adjectives or nouns used as a shorthand for complexity:

```
A beautiful disaster
A silent scream
A chaotic peace
A crowded loneliness
A violent calm
A deafening silence
A bittersweet goodbye
```

These *sound* like insight but carry none. Real complexity comes from specific contradiction in a specific scene, not from abstract noun pairings.

## 16. The "Double Noun" Title Construction

AI names experiences, emotions, and abstract states using the formula **"The [Noun] of [Noun]."** The construction sounds weighty but is almost always hollow. Examples to avoid:

```
The Architect of Pain
The Symphony of Silence
The Keeper of Secrets
The Blueprint of Tomorrow
The Garden of Regret
The Oracle of Truth
The Phantom of the Soul
```

If a line or title fits this template, it is almost certainly replaceable with something more specific.

## 17. Negation-Reveal (the "No / Just" Stanza)

AI uses anaphoric "No more / Just the" constructions, or the broader "It's not X, it's Y" / "Not X, just Y" family, to signal emotional resolution or defiance. The pattern sounds decisive but often says nothing:

```
No more running.
No more lies.
Just the truth beneath the skies.
```

The giveaway is that the resolution is always abstract ("truth," "freedom," "peace") rather than grounded in a specific action or image. Real resolution in lyrics looks like a concrete decision, not a declaration of abstract states.

### Rule

One instance of this move in a song is a strong reveal; three or more is a tic. Count occurrences across the whole lyric, not per-section, before finalizing — this applies to every phrasing of the pattern ("it's not X, it's Y" mid-verse counts the same as a stanza-opening "No more / Just the").

## 18. Title-Case Verse Headers

AI labels song sections with bracketed thematic titles — `[Verse 1: The Struggle]`, `[Bridge: The Awakening]`, `[Outro: Redemption]`. Never generate these. They force the lyric into a predetermined emotional arc and signal algorithmic structure. If section markers are needed, use plain labels only: `[Verse 1]`, `[Chorus]`, `[Bridge]`.

## Why these fail

All of these patterns share the same root problem: they describe a *concept* of a person or experience, or a *shape* a song is supposed to take, rather than an actual lived one. Real lyric speakers have specific memories, specific failures, specific embarrassing habits, and songs that don't resolve on schedule. Replace any of these patterns with a detail — or a structural choice — only this specific speaker in this specific moment could have made.

For the replacement strategy and the line-level Human Polish Filters (including Human Imperfection, the philosophical counterpart to this file), see `lyric-craft.md`.
